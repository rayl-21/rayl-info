A large dental group may still keep its provider roster in a shared spreadsheet. Most staff can read it; a small team can make changes. The file is familiar, inexpensive, and part of an established process.

Replacing it takes time. Records need checking, staff need training, and daily work must continue through the change. Keeping the spreadsheet can be a reasonable decision, even for an organization with many offices.

The calculation changes when the group wants AI to handle more insurance verification and claims work. Reading the file is only part of that task. The system also needs to know what each status means, when it applies, and what to do when the answer is unclear.

I would look closely at that gap before expanding automation. The roster shows how much useful information depends on the people and routines around it.

## One record supports several decisions

Credentialing evaluates a clinician’s professional qualifications. Contracting establishes the participation agreement. Enrollment and affiliation connect the relevant clinician, organization and practice details in the payer’s systems. Effective dates determine when the confirmed arrangement applies. These activities can overlap; they are not a universal four-step sequence.

Consider a dentist who starts working at a second office. Their qualifications may be verified, while participation at the new location remains unconfirmed. An agreement may also have a future start date.

[Delta Dental of Washington](https://www.deltadentalwa.com/provider/resources/faq.aspx) makes this distinction concrete: participation at an additional location requires a separate application. A general status such as “complete” leaves the next team with questions about which office and date it covers.

## Why a shared spreadsheet reaches its limit

An experienced coordinator may know that “complete” means an application was submitted, with payer confirmation still pending. Another colleague may use the same word to mean participation is confirmed. Staff reconcile these differences through calls, email, and memory.

AI brings that dependence into focus. A model may read a note or extract a date correctly without enough evidence to apply it to tomorrow's appointment. If staff must interpret the result every time, much of the original work remains.

The organization needs to make that context part of the shared record. Status terms need agreed meanings, and confirmations need to identify the dentist, practice, location, plan, and relevant dates. Uncertainty also needs a place: an unresolved question should remain visible and have someone responsible for answering it.

A familiar table can still be the team's interface. The important change is that software can use its records consistently and carry updates into the work that depends on them. Moving unclear records into a different application would preserve the same problem.

## Keeping the answer current is an operating responsibility

Clear records need people who can maintain them. When a dentist changes offices, someone must report the move, check the payer arrangement, and confirm when the update applies. Those responsibilities cross office management, credentialing, and billing.

A system can help connect that work. For example, it can identify an approaching license deadline, assign follow-up, and alert a manager if the issue remains open. The team still needs time, authority, and a clear process to resolve it. Sending a reminder is different from receiving and checking the renewed document.

These arrangements also shape what AI can accomplish. A tool that finds a missing confirmation needs an agreed next step and a person who can act. Otherwise, faster detection may leave the same unresolved workload waiting for attention.

That is why staff training and responsibility belong in the implementation plan. People need to know how to report a change, question an answer, and resolve conflicting information. They also need to see whether an update reached the other teams that use it.

## How the same answer reaches verification and claims

Return to the dentist working at a second office. Before tomorrow's appointment, the verification workflow needs the participation status for that dentist, office, plan, and treatment date. If confirmation is missing, the system can route that question to its owner while patient benefit checks continue.

The two checks serve different purposes. Provider participation does not establish a patient's eligibility, remaining benefits, deductibles, treatment limits, or authorization requirements. The [ADA's review of eligibility and benefits verification](https://www.ada.org/-/media/project/ada-organization/ada/ada-org/files/resources/practice/dental-insurance/eligibility_and_benefits_verification.pdf) describes the gaps practices must resolve across different information sources.

Once the payer confirms participation, the verification team needs that answer and its effective date. The update may also require a review of the patient estimate. Software is useful here when it brings the confirmed answer into the team's normal work, with the evidence available for review.

After treatment, the claims team can use the same provider record when preparing the submission. The claim still needs the actual clinician, billing entity, location, and services provided. A mismatch should prompt review before it becomes another correction to manage.

The connection continues through claim acceptance, payment review, and follow-up. If provider information caused a problem, the correction should reach other affected work. A current roster supports this process; it does not guarantee payment.

## Scale changes the coordination required

The same problem takes different forms as a practice grows. These size ranges are planning examples; provider count, payer arrangements, and legal entities also affect the workload.

In a **solo practice or one office**, much of the knowledge may sit with one busy person. A maintained record and a backup who understands it can reduce that dependence. The necessary tools may fit within existing software or a managed service.

Across **2–10 locations**, coordination includes keeping office assignments and payer information consistent as dentists move. A shared record helps when each office knows how to report changes and someone takes responsibility for resolving them.

At **11–50 locations**, more work passes between separate teams. A confirmation received by credentialing must reach the people checking appointments and preparing claims. Shared case tracking can make those handoffs and outstanding questions visible.

With **more than 50 locations**, acquisitions and bulk changes add further demands. Common definitions and update rules help teams use the same information across systems. Managers also need a way to assign unresolved work without losing important differences between offices.

Across these settings, automation depends on how well information and responsibility move together. Adding AI to one team's task may offer little relief if the next team still rebuilds the answer.

## What I would change first

I would start with a small group of offices and follow one provider update from the roster through verification and claims. That would show where staff repeat checks, wait for another team, or use different versions of the answer.

The next step would be to agree on the record's meaning and who maintains it, then connect it to those workflows. During the transition, staff need time to learn the process and a clear rule for which record to use. Their feedback should shape the change.

Same-day or next-day verification is a useful target, but it also depends on patient information, payer responses, and capacity to resolve exceptions. I would measure completion before appointments, staff time per case, and provider-related claim corrections. Cases still waiting at the deadline belong in that assessment too.

Dental offers a concrete example of a wider issue in outpatient care. An organization can give AI access to information before it has agreed how that information should guide the work. Reliable records, clear responsibility, and software that connects teams give it a better basis to act. The result is worth judging by how much work staff no longer have to repeat.
