Let’s strip away the breathless national security headlines and call Jensen Huang’s departure from Nvidia what it actually is: a coordinated political attack. Jensen Huang was not fired for treason, nor was he caught leaking company secrets. He [resigned on November 12](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0001045810/000104581026000082/nvidiaannouncesceotransiti.htm) under the crushing weight of a strategically leaked, uncorroborated espionage narrative engineered by government officials and congressional China hawks determined to destroy his influence over U.S. chip policy. And while racial animus may not have been the initial spark that lit this fire, the aftermath has laid bare an ugly reality: when Washington wants to bring down a tech titan of Chinese descent, the fallout weaponizes race in a way that casts a shadow of suspicion over every Asian American and Chinese American in this country.

## Fourteen Days of Orchestrated Leaks

For a year after The New York Times first reported on Singapore cloud firm Megaspeed in October 2025, the story was strictly about export controls and potential chip diversion. Then, on October 29, 2026, a fourteen-day media avalanche began. Anonymous government sources suddenly fed The New York Times a narrative that Megaspeed executive Alice Huang was a “Chinese intelligence asset” targeting the Nvidia CEO.

From there, the leaks operated like a coordinated relay race:

- **October 30:** The Wall Street Journal claimed Singapore and Taiwan officials suspected Alice Huang of espionage—a claim Singapore’s Ministry of Home Affairs [publicly rebuked](https://www.police.gov.sg/media-hub/news/2026/10/20261030_clarification_statement_on_wall_street_journals_article), clarifying she was only questioned for false declarations and “not investigated for espionage.”
- **November 4–7:** House committee chairs Brian Mast, John Moolenaar, and Rick Crawford demanded a closed-door briefing, while Senators Chris Coons and Elizabeth Warren ordered federal records preserved. Simultaneously, The New York Times and Financial Times leaked a classified memo alleging China’s Ministry of State Security (MSS) recruited Alice Huang and revealing the relationship was romantic.
- **November 8:** Jensen Huang apologized to staff for infidelity, while Nvidia’s board opened an independent investigation, maintaining there was “no reason to believe that either national security or Nvidia secrets were compromised.”
- **November 11–12:** The New York Times dropped an unrelated human-resources story about a relationship with an internal executive, Kimberly Powell, and the Journal reported the board was preparing to oust him over corporate governance. Boxed in by two weeks of relentless pressure, Huang resigned.

## The “Spy” Story Collapses—Right on Cue

The most damning proof that this was a political hit job came the exact moment its goal was achieved. On November 12 and 13—once Huang was safely out the door—the entire intelligence premise began to unravel.

CNN reported that the CIA was not convinced Alice Huang spied for China and that U.S. intelligence officials thought the “spy” label was overstated. Attorney General Todd Blanche publicly confirmed the Justice Department had reached “no conclusion” that she was a spy. Even President Trump remarked, “The big thing is, was the woman a spy, and I don’t know if that’s been determined yet.”

Most tellingly, The Washington Post revealed on November 13 that the whole firestorm traced back to a “shadow war for Trump’s ear on China.” Secretary of State Marco Rubio had taken an underlying CIA report and recast it “in his own more assertive terms” in a March 30 memo. As one former official told the Post, China hawks viewed Huang’s personal indiscretion as a “nuclear bomb” they could detonate because “they wanted to destroy the influence Jensen had over the president.” Huang had previously called the label “China hawk” a “badge of shame.” In response, Washington hawks used unverified intelligence leaks to take his scalp and push new chip restrictions into the defense bill.

## The Racial Collateral Damage

Jensen Huang made personal mistakes in his private life and marriage—flaws he openly admitted. In any normal corporate crisis, an affair is handled between a CEO, his family, and his board.

Instead, government officials and lawmakers immediately inflated a personal failing into an act of Beijing espionage without proof. When sitting members of Congress and intelligence leakers treat any association with a Chinese national as automatic proof of a Communist Party plot, the collateral damage extends far beyond Nvidia’s C-suite.

Politics drove the takedown, not race—but race is defining the aftermath. By weaponizing unverified “honeytrap” tropes and treating ethnic ties as national security liabilities, Washington has revived a toxic climate of perpetual foreignness. If the immigrant founder of a four-trillion-dollar American powerhouse can be publicly branded a national security threat on intelligence that the CIA and DOJ won’t even stand behind, every Asian American—and especially every Chinese American—in technology, science, and leadership is left with a chilling message: in Washington’s new Cold War, you are always one rumor away from being treated as the enemy within.
